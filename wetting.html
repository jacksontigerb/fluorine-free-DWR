What to look for

Worth trying.

  • Roughness cuts both ways. Start on uncoated cotton and turn the roughness up. The drop spreads faster, not slower.
  • My coating against the commercial one. Same texture, ten degrees of chemistry apart, almost the same contact angle. One sits on air and rolls off, the other sinks into the weave and stays put.
  • Sparse beats deep. Drop the solid contact towards the lotus setting and the roll-off angle collapses.

Why this is here. Every coating I made beads water at about 130°, level with the commercial finish, yet only the commercial one shed a small droplet. The same contact angle can be reached from either state, and only one of them lets the drop go.

Under the hood

The equations behind it.

  • Wenzel, 1936 cosΘ* = r·cosθ

    The drop sinks into the texture. Roughness multiplies whatever the flat surface already does, so it makes a repellent surface more repellent and a wetting surface wetter.

  • Cassie and Baxter, 1944 cosΘ* = fs(cosθ + 1) − 1

    The drop bridges the texture and rests partly on trapped air. Whichever state has the lower energy wins, and air takes over above cosθ < (fs − 1)/(r − fs), marked on the first slider. Below that line a drop can still perch on air, until pressure or a wash pushes it in.

  • Furmidge, 1962 ρVg·sinα = γw(cosθrec − cosθadv)

    Roll-off is a force balance: gravity down the slope against the grip of the drop's edge. Grip is taken to scale with how much solid the drop touches, from a smooth-surface baseline of 0.30.

  • The caveat idealised

    This is a square-pillar array, not a woven fabric. Real cotton grips harder than any regular texture, so the model rolls drops off more easily than I measured. It gets the ordering and the mechanism right, which is what it is here for.
