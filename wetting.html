Wetting concepts

Why contact angle is not enough.

In my results, finishes with almost the same contact angle behaved very differently once the fabric was tilted, and washing changed how water ran off far more than it changed the angle. This page shows why that is possible, with two ideas: the same angle can come from two different ways of wetting, and whether a drop runs off depends on angles the static test does not measure.

Textbook models on idealised surfaces. They do not describe my fabrics, and nothing here says which state my coatings or the commercial finish are in; that was not measured. The measured results are on the results page.

Same angle, two explanations.

Pick a smooth surface angle for the material and the contact angle you measured on the rough version. There are two ideal ways to get there. The drop can fill the texture, or it can rest on the tips with air trapped underneath. Both give exactly the same angle, so the angle alone cannot tell them apart.

°

The angle a perfectly smooth sheet of the same material would give. The highest measured on any smooth solid is about 119°, on close packed CF3 groups. Hydrocarbon surfaces sit below that. 108° is a starting point, not a measurement of my coating.

What makes a drop let go.

Tilt a surface and gravity pulls the drop downhill. What holds it back is the difference between the angle at its front edge as it tries to advance and the angle at its back edge as it tries to recede. A static contact angle measures neither. Set the two angles and the drop size, and the balance gives the tilt at which the drop starts to move.

°

The largest angle the front edge reaches before it moves.

°

The smallest angle the back edge reaches before it lets go. It cannot be above the advancing angle. A back edge that grips the surface means a low receding angle.

µL

What to look for

Worth trying.

  • Two routes to 132°. In the first model, with the smooth surface at 108°, a measured 132° needs a roughness of 2.17 if the drop fills the texture, or 48% solid contact if it rests on air.
  • Same drop, different grip. In the second model at 10 µL, advancing 150° and receding 110° releases at about 55°. Raise the receding angle to 130° and it goes at about 17°. A static angle, which sits somewhere between the two, could look much the same in both cases.
  • Drop size changes the answer. With the starting angles, a 5 µL drop does not release by 90°, 10 µL goes at about 55°, and 33 µL at about 22°.

Under the hood

The equations and their limits.

  • Wenzel, 1936 cosθ* = r·cosθY

    The liquid follows every bump of the surface. Roughness multiplies whatever the smooth surface already does, pushing the angle further from 90° in whichever direction it already leans. doi.org

  • Cassie and Baxter, 1944 cosθ* = fs(cosθY + 1) − 1

    The drop rests on the tops with air beneath, so only a fraction fs of its base touches solid. In the ideal picture, resting on air has the lower energy when cosθY < (fs − 1)/(r − fs). doi.org

  • Furmidge, 1962 ρVg·sinα = kγw(cosθR − cosθA)

    A force balance between the drop's weight down the slope and the grip of its edge. The footprint width w comes from a spherical cap at the average of the two angles, which gets less accurate as gravity flattens the drop (a Bond number above about 0.3). The factor k depends on drop shape; ElSherbini and Jacobi measured how. Furmidge · ElSherbini and Jacobi, 2006

  • The smooth surface ceiling ≈ 119°

    The highest water contact angle reported on a smooth solid, on close packed CF3 groups. Nishino et al., 1999

  • What none of this covers idealised

    A woven fabric is not a regular array of pillars. It has roughness at two scales (fibres and yarns), it bends, and cotton soaks water up. These models show the ideas behind the measurements. They are not fitted to my samples, and they cannot say which state a drop on my fabric was in.
